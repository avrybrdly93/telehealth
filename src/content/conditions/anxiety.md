This page is educational and isn't a diagnosis. A psychiatric evaluation is how we understand your specific situation.

## What anxiety can feel like

Everyone feels worried sometimes. Before a big presentation, a hard conversation, a medical visit — that's normal. An anxiety disorder is different. The worry or fear is more constant, more intense, or shows up when there's no real reason for it. And it starts getting in the way of daily life.

That can look like a lot of things. Some people feel constant worry about everyday matters — work, health, money. It doesn't ease up even when things are fine. Others notice physical symptoms first. A racing heart. A tight chest. Shortness of breath. An upset stomach. Tense muscles. Some people have sudden, intense panic that peaks within minutes. Others feel anxious mainly around other people. They worry about being judged. So they start avoiding gatherings, calls, or new faces. Sleep is often affected too — racing thoughts at bedtime, or waking up already on edge.

Anxiety disorders are common. About 18% of U.S. adults had one in the past year, per the National Institute of Mental Health. Roughly a third of adults have one at some point in their lives ([NIMH, Anxiety Disorders](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/anxiety-disorders)). Having an anxiety disorder isn't a sign of weakness. It isn't a failure to "handle things." It's a recognized, treatable condition.

## How psychiatric care typically helps

A psychiatric evaluation looks at what you're experiencing, how long it's been going on, and how it affects your day-to-day life. From there, your provider helps build a plan around that. Depending on your situation, a plan may include:

- **Medication management.** For many people, medication can ease the intensity of anxiety symptoms — racing thoughts, physical tension, panic. That can make daily life, and other coping strategies, more workable. Whether medication fits you is worked out individually with your provider. This page isn't recommending anything specific.
- **Coordination with therapy.** Approaches like cognitive behavioral therapy are often paired with medication management for anxiety. If you have, or want, a therapist, your provider can coordinate care around that.
- **Ongoing follow-up.** Anxiety symptoms can shift over time. Follow-up visits let your provider adjust the plan as things change, rather than setting it once and walking away.

## Next step

If this sounds like what you've been feeling, a psychiatric evaluation is the next step. A provider reviews your specific history and symptoms with you — not a generic checklist — and helps build a plan from there.
