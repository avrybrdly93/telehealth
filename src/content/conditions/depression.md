This page is educational and isn't a diagnosis. A psychiatric evaluation is how we understand your specific situation.

## What depression can feel like

Depression is more than a rough week or a bad mood. For many people, it's a low or flat mood that sticks around most of the day, most days, for two weeks or longer. It can also show up as losing interest in things you used to enjoy. Changes in appetite or weight. Trouble sleeping, or sleeping too much. Feeling tired even after rest. Trouble concentrating. Feeling worthless or unusually guilty. Moving or speaking more slowly than usual. Some people notice mainly physical changes — low energy, aches, appetite shifts — before they connect any of it to their mood.

Depression doesn't look the same for everyone. Some people cry easily. Others feel numb or "flat" and can't explain why. Some keep functioning at work or school while feeling exhausted underneath. Others find it hard to get out of bed. None of these versions is more "real" than another. None of them is a personal failing. Depression is a recognized medical condition, not a character flaw. It affects people across every background and life circumstance.

The National Institute of Mental Health estimates that 8.3% of U.S. adults had at least one major depressive episode in the past year ([NIMH, Major Depression statistics](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/statistics/major-depression)). That's millions of people navigating exactly this.

## How psychiatric care typically helps

A psychiatric evaluation starts with your history and current symptoms — what's been happening, for how long, and how it's affecting your daily life. From there, care is built around what fits your situation. That can include:

- **Medication management.** For many people, medication can ease symptoms like low mood, poor sleep, or low energy. That can make it more possible to engage in daily life, and in therapy. Whether medication makes sense for you is worked out individually with your provider. This page doesn't recommend specific medications.
- **Coordination with therapy.** Medication management and talk therapy often work well together. If you're already seeing a therapist, or want a referral, your provider can coordinate around that.
- **Ongoing follow-up.** Depression care is rarely a single visit. Follow-up appointments let your provider check how things are going and adjust the plan as needed.

## If you're in crisis right now

If depression has brought on thoughts of suicide or self-harm, or you're in crisis right now, please don't wait for a scheduled appointment. See the crisis resources below — they're available any time.
